UK online gift wholesaler, 2009-12-01 to 2011-12-09. 1,067,371 transaction lines, £20.11M of merchandise sales. Generated by `python -m retail`; numbers match the dashboard.

## 1. Sales grew through bigger orders, not more of them

Merchandise sales reached £10.06M in the twelve months to November 2011, up 6.7% on the year before. Orders fell 4.0%, so all of the growth came from average order value, which rose 11.2%.

**What to do:** Track order value by customer segment to confirm whether larger baskets come from existing wholesalers or a shift in customer mix.

## 2. The business lives and dies by autumn

September to November delivered 36% of the year's sales. November 2011 alone was £1.46M, 1.5% above November 2010. Stock for the Christmas range has to be committed by late summer.

**What to do:** Plan inventory and staffing around the Q4 ramp; this is where forecast accuracy pays most.

## 3. A UK business with a few very large overseas accounts

The UK accounts for 86% of sales. Outside it, value is concentrated: 3 identified customers in Ireland bought £629k, with an average order of £1,082.

**What to do:** Treat the top overseas accounts as key accounts with named owners; losing one is material.

## 4. Orders arrive Monday to Friday lunchtime, plus Sunday

Thursday is the busiest day and 12:00 the busiest hour. Saturday trading is almost non-existent (30 orders in two years), while Sunday is a normal trading day.

**What to do:** Align staffing with observed ordering hours. Test promotion timing separately before claiming it improves sales.

## 5. A fifth of customers drive three quarters of revenue

Champions are 1,508 customers (26%) but 71% of identified revenue. 397 'at risk' customers used to order often (median 5 orders) but have been quiet for 359 days; they spent £1.37M in total.

**What to do:** Run a win-back campaign for the at-risk segment before spending on acquisition.

## 6. Compare retention at the same cohort maturity

Across 11 cohorts with twelve complete follow-up months, 20% ordered in month one and 18% in month twelve. These are monthly activity rates for the same cohorts, not the probability of staying after a second purchase. The initial left-censored cohort is excluded.

**What to do:** Test an early reorder reminder against a control group; measure repeat orders and incremental value.

## 7. A long tail of slow sellers

1,024 of 4,707 products produce 80% of sales. The bottom 2,423 products ('C' class) share just 5%.

**What to do:** Review C-class lines alongside margin, stock and range requirements before proposing delisting.

## 8. Customers buy in matching sets

The strongest pairing is Pink Polkadot Cup with Blue Polkadot Cup: bought together 521 times, 36 times more often than chance. Most top pairs are colour variants of the same item.

**What to do:** Sell colour variants as bundles and place them together online.

## 9. Large credit adjustments need separate investigation

Credits below the size threshold total 2.2% of sales; including large adjustments, credits total 3.6%. Large adjustments account for £287k. The flag means at least 1,000 units or £10,000 per line; it does not prove a cancellation or physical return.

**What to do:** Reconcile large credits to their original invoices before assigning a cause. Review sensitivity to the size threshold.

## 10. Forecast accuracy on a separate holdout

On a holdout of five forecast weeks before Christmas 2011, gradient boosting had a weighted error of 46% against 53% for a 4-week average. Its signed bias was -18%, and the nominal 80% quantile range held 75% of actual outcomes.

**What to do:** Use the quantile range in a replenishment scenario. Safety stock requires lead times, service targets and inventory costs, which this dataset lacks.

## 11. Recommendations: blend 'similar' with 'trending'

For returning customers, a top-10 list blending purchase similarity with current trends contained a product they went on to buy for the first time 44% of the time, against 18% for best sellers and 43% for trending alone, while covering 27% of the catalogue (trending: 3%).

**What to do:** Use the hybrid for 'you may also like' and reorder emails; A/B test before claiming uplift.

## Caveats

- Sales are gross merchandise sales in GBP. There is no cost, margin or stock data, so nothing here is profit.
- 13% of sales have no customer ID; customer analysis covers identified customers only.
- December 2009 contains customers who were active before the data starts, so that cohort is not truly new.
- Model results come from one retailer and one Christmas season. They are a limited historical evaluation, not a guaranteed uplift.
